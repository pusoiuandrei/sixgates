# Buffett and Munger: The Operating Recipe (reference for the product team)

Source: the user's reference document, built from the Berkshire letters 1977-2024, the Owner's Manual (1996), the Buffett Partnership letters, Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville (1984), the 1998 University of Florida talk, the 2010 FCIC interview, Munger's USC 1994 / Harvard 1995 / USC 2007 talks, Poor Charlie's Almanack checklist, Abel's 2025 letter (Feb 2026). Letter years refer to the Berkshire letter for that year.

One-sentence method: buy pieces of a few understandable businesses that earn high returns on little capital behind a durable moat, pay clearly less than a conservative estimate of the cash they will produce over their lifetime, and keep them for as long as those facts hold.

## The goal
- Compound after-tax purchasing power per unit of capital over decades, with probability of permanent loss near zero. Not: beat the market every year, be right often, be busy.
- Investing defined (2011): giving up purchasing power now for the reasoned expectation of more purchasing power later, after tax. Cash and bonds are not the safe asset by default over long horizons (2011, 2014).
- Risk defined: reasoned probability of a loss of purchasing power over the holding period (2011); possibility of loss or injury (1993). Not volatility, not beta. Five risk drivers (1993): certainty about long-term economics; certainty about management ability; certainty management passes rewards to owners; purchase price; future taxation and inflation.
- Berkshire objective: maximise average annual gain in intrinsic value per share, never size (1983 principles; Owner's Manual; Abel 2025). Intrinsic value = discounted value of cash that can be taken out of a business over its remaining life.
- Measuring rules: partnership ground rules (1956, restated Jan 1963): no return guaranteed; a good year beats the Dow and leading funds whether absolute result is plus or minus; 3 years minimum to judge, 5 preferred; no market prediction. Berkshire: 5-year rolling test vs S&P 500 with dividends; every retained dollar must create at least a dollar of market value or be paid out (1983; Owner's Manual).
- Permanent loss vs quotational loss: Berkshire stock fell 59%, 37%, 49%, 51% in four episodes (2017). Structure so that no decline forces a sale.
- About a dozen truly good decisions in 58 years, one every five years (2022). Munger: a few mispriced bets, bet big when odds favour you (USC 1994).
- Honest fallback: if you cannot beat a low-cost S&P 500 index fund after costs and taxes over a decade, own the index (2013 will instructions: 90% S&P 500 fund, 10% short-term governments; 2016 bet).
- Objective function in order: never be forced to sell; compound after-tax purchasing power; judge only on rolling five-year results against the index.

## The mindset (16 rules)
1. A share is a piece of a business; evaluate as if buying the whole company; business-pickers not stock-pickers (1977, 2013, 2022).
2. Mr. Market serves you, does not guide you; a quote is an option to transact, never information about value (1987).
3. Margin of safety is the cornerstone; the harder to value, the bigger the discount; price is what you pay, value is what you get (1992, 1984 Superinvestors, 2008).
4. Circle of competence: size does not matter, knowing the boundary is vital; piles: in, out, too hard (1996; Munger).
5. Temperament over intellect: consistently not stupid; avoiding dragons beats slaying them; standard causes of misjudgment (incentives, social proof, commitment, loss aversion, availability, authority, envy); checklists (1989; Munger Harvard 1995; Almanack).
6. Inversion: ask what would ruin you and avoid it (Munger USC 2007; Almanack).
7. Inactivity is intelligent: lethargy bordering on sloth; twenty-punch card (1990, 1996, Munger 1994).
8. Concentrate when you know: 5 to 10 businesses for a know-something investor (1993); partnership went to 40% in one idea when near-certain (1966); diversification is protection against ignorance (1996 meeting).
9. Opportunity cost is the hurdle: every idea competes with the best thing you own and with the index (Almanack).
10. Independence: right because facts and reasoning are right, not because others agree.
11. No macro, no timing: forecasts are an expensive distraction; price purchases rather than time them (1994); forecasters fill your ear, never your wallet (2014).
12. Welcome lower prices if a net buyer (1997 hamburger test); pessimism is the most common cause of low prices, optimism the enemy of the rational buyer (1990).
13. Widespread fear is your friend, personal fear your enemy (2016); the less prudent others are, the more prudent you must be (2017).
14. Review mistakes then fix fast; the cardinal sin is delaying correction (2024); omissions cost more than commissions (1989, 2008).
15. Learning machine: wisdom acquisition is a moral duty; 80 or 90 models carry most of the freight (Munger 2007, 1994).
16. Deserve what you want; work only with people you like, trust and admire; never a good deal with a bad person (1989; Munger 2007).
Two layers: Graham (price vs value, margin of safety, Mr. Market) and Munger (fair price for a great business, moats, scale, psychology); second dominates at scale, first still decides what you pay (2014).

## How to analyse a company (the method)
Filter since 1977: a business you understand, favourable long-term economics, honest and able people, sensible price. First three are gates; price last; most candidates die at gate one.
Candidates come from reading (annual reports, 10-Ks across an industry, competitors' reports, trade journals) and scuttlebutt (customers, suppliers, competitors; ask CEOs which rival they would buy). Anything sold with a large commission and a thick prospectus is excluded (Munger 1994).
1. Gate: can you say what the business looks like in ten years? If complex or subject to constant change, Buffett does not try to predict its cash flows (1992). Fast-changing industries excluded as a class (1996, 2007). If you cannot state the reason in one paragraph, do not buy (Florida 1998). Too-hard pile.
2. Economics: pricing power first (FCIC 2010: single most important question; if raising price needs a prayer session, terrible business). Return on tangible capital, not reported EPS (acquisition criteria; 2017 wording: good returns on net tangible assets required to operate; 1977: judge by ROE not EPS growth). Capital intensity great/good/gruesome (2007): See's $25m price 1972, ~$8m net tangible assets, <$5m pre-tax; 2007 $82m pre-tax on $40m capital; only $32m reinvested in 35 years, $1.35bn sent to Omaha; FlightSafety $509m extra capital for $159m extra pre-tax (good); airlines gruesome. Growth worth paying for only when incremental capital earns well above its cost (1992, 2000). Owner earnings (1986): reported earnings + depreciation, amortisation, other non-cash charges, minus average annual maintenance capex (to maintain competitive position and unit volume), adjusted for working capital; not EBITDA. Demonstrated consistent earning power over a long record; projections and turnarounds of no interest; turnarounds seldom turn (1979). Survival: little or no debt; stress test like Wells Fargo 1990 (assume 10% of loans go bad with 30% loss severity; does it still break even?). Inflation favours asset-light businesses with real economic goodwill (1983 appendix).
3. Moat: enduring moat protecting high returns on capital because competitors attack any profitable castle (2007); accepted moats: low-cost producer (GEICO, Costco) or powerful worldwide brand (Coca-Cola, Gillette); Munger: scale advantages in cost, information, social proof; surfing a long wave. Franchise test (1991): needed or desired; no close substitute in customers' minds; price not regulated; a franchise survives mismanagement, a plain business earns exceptional profits only as low-cost operator or in a supply squeeze. Capital test: give a capable rival a very large sum to take the business away; if they could, no moat (Florida 1998). Needs a star manager = not great (2007). Inevitables vs impostors (1996). Watch direction, not level (1991 newspapers).
4. Management: candour (report what you would want if roles reversed, 1983 principle 12; published mistakes 1989, 2024); capital allocation record (one-dollar test; buybacks only below intrinsic value, what is smart at one price is stupid at another: 1983, 2011, 2016; shares issued only for equal value; no empire building); resistance to the institutional imperative (1989: resisting change, projects absorbing cash, studies rubber-stamping the leader, imitating peers); integrity, intelligence, energy (Kiewit rule); jockey vs horse (1989); Buffett bought Moody's without meeting management (FCIC 2010).
5. Valuation: intrinsic value = cash taken out over remaining life, discounted (1992 Williams; Owner's Manual); Aesop (2000): how sure are the birds in the bush, when, how many, risk-free rate. Inputs: conservative owner earnings; long government bond yield as hurdle; no risk premium bolted on (risk handled by certainty gate and discount demanded); no false precision; approximately right beats precisely wrong (1993); if not obvious without a spreadsheet, not obvious. Pay for quality but still pay a price: wonderful company at fair price (1989); See's $25m ceiling (1991); even inevitables can be overpriced (1996); Coca-Cola at 50x in 1998 a silly price he should have sold at (2006 meeting; 2004 letter). Margin sized to uncertainty: not $83m for $80m, an enormous margin (1984). Cross-checks: owner-earnings yield vs long bond; return on tangible capital vs multiple of tangible book paid; portfolio look-through earnings yield (1991).
6. Decision record: one paragraph before buying: what the business looks like in ten years, why the moat holds, what you pay vs what it is worth, what would prove you wrong. Almanack checklist: risk, independence, preparation, intellectual humility, analytic rigour, allocation, patience, decisiveness, change, focus. Then wait for the price.

## How to buy
Price last; never pulls you into a worse business. Wait for the fat pitch (1997 Ted Williams; cannot be called out); twenty-punch card. Buy on pessimism (1990); best purchases were great businesses with temporary solvable problems (AmEx 1964, GEICO 1976, Wells Fargo 1990, Apple 2016 at 10-14x). Price, do not time (1994); only aggregate check: interest rates like gravity, corporate profits/GDP mean-revert (Fortune 1999, 2001), a sanity check not a timing tool. Size for conviction: 5-10 businesses; up to 40% when near-certain (1966). Buy more as price falls if thesis intact (Wells Fargo 1990; 2011 wish for a languishing price); never average down into a deteriorating business (1979). No borrowed money (2014; 2017 drawdown table; LTCM parable). Hold cash as the price of being able to act, not as a forecast (2008; Abel 2025); when nothing meets the tests, sit (1986); cash is a parking place not a home (2008); default residence of capital is productive assets (2011). Buy only what you could hold through a 50% quotational decline. If you will not do the work: low-cost S&P 500 index fund bought steadily (2013, 2016).

## How to own
Own like a farm with no daily quote (2013). Monitor two things: are the economics preserved; is management still able and honest (1996). Think in look-through earnings; aim for the highest look-through earnings a decade out (1990, 1991). Let winners run; do not cut the flowers and water the weeds (1988); weeds wither in significance (2022). Use tax deferral (1989 arithmetic: $1 doubling 20 times, one tax at end about $692,000 vs about $25,000 with tax at each doubling at 34%). Ignore cost basis. Ignore forecasts, politics, rates, pundits. Prefer buybacks below intrinsic value to dividends while retained dollars create more than a dollar (1983, 2011, 2016). Re-estimate intrinsic value once a year; volatility is far from synonymous with risk (2014).

## How to sell
Forever is the default for an outstanding business with outstanding management (1988), not a law. Sell only when: (1) you were wrong (fix at once, 2024); (2) economics deteriorated structurally not cyclically (1991 newspapers); (3) management lost integrity or destroys value (1989); (4) price far beyond any reasonable value and a clearly better use after tax exists (2004 letter, 2006 meeting on Coca-Cola); (5) you need the money for your life (size correctly up front). Never sell because price fell, a forecast, a pundit, locking in gains, long holding period, appreciation (1987). Averaging down is for sound businesses at panic prices. Errors of omission (Disney 1966) cost more than commission (1989, 2008). Wholly owned subsidiaries: stricter rule, no gin-rummy discarding (1983).

## The cadence
Daily: read four to six hours (annual reports, 10-Ks, trade journals, newspapers, business history, other disciplines); keep the calendar nearly empty; ask why, why, why; say no to almost everything. Not: check prices as information, read forecasts, trade, fill the day with meetings.
Weekly: work one business or industry to the bottom (ten years of reports, competitors, scuttlebutt); sort in / too hard / out; update watchlist with value range and the price you would pay; write or revise one one-page thesis.
Monthly: read reports for what changed in the business (volumes, pricing, returns on capital, debt, capital allocation); check liquidity so nothing is forced; log mistakes of omission and commission.
Yearly: reread every holding's annual report; re-estimate intrinsic value ranges; re-run moat and management checks; one-dollar test on each company's retained earnings and your own; write a letter to yourself (rolling five-year result vs index after tax and costs, mistakes, what you learned); if you lag the index over a decade, buy the index.
Lifelong: widen the circle slowly; study failures; protect the compounding engine (no leverage, no ruin, no fraud, reputation intact); work only with people you admire; stay a learning machine; start early and live long.

## The anti-recipe
No macro or market forecasts, timing, technical analysis. No borrowed money against stocks, no shorting as strategy, no derivatives speculation. No businesses outside the circle, no fast-changing industries, no turnarounds, no sellers' projections, no auctions, no unfriendly takeovers. No diversification for its own sake (Noah school). No EBITDA, no adjusted earnings, no repeated restructuring charges treated as one-offs. No precision theatre (beta, CAPM, degree-of-difficulty points). No fees to managers who do not beat the index after costs. No selling because prices fell; no buying because prices rose. No gin-rummy discarding; no dividends while a retained dollar earns more than a dollar. No risking what you have and need for what you do not need. No reputation risk for a return.

## What is not transferable
Insurance float (about 1.6-1.7x leverage at a cost below T-bills; Buffett's Alpha, FAJ 2018): the quality-and-price tilt is usable, float is not; margin debt is not a substitute. Permanent capital (no redemptions): the equivalent is no leverage, a liquidity reserve, position sizes you can hold through the worst year. Deal flow and reputation. Size in the individual's favour (small sums, no career risk, no calendar). Two Buffetts: 1957-1968 partnership 31.6%/yr before fees (cigar butts, workouts, controls); quality approach began with See's 1972 under Munger. Berkshire now: Abel CEO from 1 Jan 2026; same rules restated in Feb 2026 letter (concentration, patience, buybacks only below conservatively estimated intrinsic value, no dividends while the one-dollar test holds, cash as a strategic asset).

## Worked example (Coca-Cola 1988-2026)
Piece: about 7% bought 1988-1994, about 9.3% today without buying a share since 1994 (400m shares, cost $1.3bn). Understand: sold Coke in 1936, read old reports incl. 1896, could describe the business ten years out. Returns on capital: concentrate model, bottlers hold heavy assets; 2025 dividends paid $8.8bn on about $12bn net income. Moat: inevitable 1996; 2026: 64th consecutive dividend increase, Q2 2026 volume +5% with margins up. Price: about 15x after the 1987 crash; 7% earnings yield + 15% growth, >20%/yr first decade. Hold: 38 years; 2026 dividend to Berkshire 400m x $2.12 = $848m = 65% of cost per year; market value $32.5bn at 30 June 2026 = 25x cost. Mistake: not selling at 50x in 1998; 1998-2013 total return about 2%/yr. Lesson: holding right for the business, wrong for the price for fifteen years; price is one of the facts to check.
